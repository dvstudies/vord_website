We have intentionally designed a method that remains very simple and underdefined, precisely to provide room for more unmediated and useful results.  In our Show & Tell sessions, researchers brings one to three images and reflects, without a script, on how they think with them, followed by a moderated discussion. Each session is, in the anthropological sense, a thick description of a single researcher's visual reasoning performed live. The goal is not the ultimate authoritative interpretation of the image, but the explication of an usually invisible choreography between the researcher's gaze, their conceptual vocabulary, and the affordances of the image.

The format stands on several theoretical shoulders:

**Bildwissenschaft.** Boehm's iconic difference insists that images signify in ways irreducible to language; Bredekamp's *Bildakt* argues that images are not passive representations but active forces shaping cognition. When a participant pauses, zooms in, returns to a detail, or leaps from a formal observation to a historical analogy, the *Bildakt* becomes audible in real time: the image is doing something to their thinking, and they are doing something to the image.

**Visual studies.** Mitchell's call for a "showing seeing" — a meta-critical attention to how we look — and Elkins's insistence that our field account for its own failures of attention give the workshop its reflexive frame. Show & Tell is a performance of showing seeing: an *explication de texte* applied to the researcher's own cognitive process.

**Epistemology of practice.** Schön's reflective practitioner and recent work on the epistemology of images remind us that knowledge is formed through material encounter, in time, through the body — not through abstract inference alone.


Over time, the accumulated sessions will not produce a taxonomy. They produce something closer to Warburg's *Bilderatlas*: a montage of heterogeneous encounters whose meaning emerges through juxtaposition, not classification, and where patterns appear inductively.

And that is exactly what a project like ours needs. A model trained for humanistic "image reasoning" does not need a labelled dataset of methods. It needs a corpus of *performed thinking* — traces of how scholars move from the visual to the conceptual, from the singular to the general, from the image to the argument. with Show & Tell we test  precisely that,  a sort of living archive of humanistic visual cognition, which we will document through consented transcripts, analytical diagrams, and enriched image annotations. This is aimed at the community of visual culture scholars who will work with AI, now or in the future.

The ultimate aim of the project is to learn to describe, and eventually support, the complementary modes, capacities, and ways of looking that humans and models each bring to the image.
