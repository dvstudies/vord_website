The third Thinking with Images workshop took place on Friday, 9 October 2026, 11:00–13:00, at DVS, Culmannstrasse 1, Zurich. Continuing the format developed in the earlier sessions, participants used a small number of images to reconstruct how visual materials actually enter their research: what they notice, what questions images generate, where interpretation encounters resistance, and how images shift between object, evidence, method, and conceptual device. During each presentation, a live hand-drawn diagram recorded these movements; afterwards, the recordings and diagrams were transcribed, annotated, and enhanced to identify recurring epistemic operations such as thinking ON, ABOUT, WITH, and AGAINST images.


The three presentations substantially broadened the range of cases. Adil Bokhary worked through Botticelli’s Primavera, moving from architectural questions of reading and seeing to the painting as database, garden, and theatre. Computational segmentation, uncertain flower identification, generative recomposition, and estrangement raised questions of provenance, semantic “resolution,” productive error, and distributed authorship. 

Thinking with Images 3 _ Adil B… Javier Argota examined black-hole imagery across theoretical simulation, cinematic visualization, and composite scientific imaging, foregrounding indirect visibility, constructed evidence, and the role of technical and aesthetic mediation. Tristan Weddigen, through two Caspar Wolf landscapes, explored how historical images can become evidence for changing environments while remaining pictorial constructions requiring comparison, calibration, and critical reconstruction of viewpoint.
Compared with earlier workshops—where classification, description, circulation, and cataloguing uncertainty were especially prominent—this session shifted attention toward generation, scientific visualization, and diachronic evidence. A key takeaway is that images rarely occupy a single epistemic role: they move through chains of observation, manipulation, resistance, comparison, and reinterpretation. Particularly productive moments occur where images fail to align neatly with categories, data, sites, or expectations; these mismatches often generate the next research question rather than simply constituting errors.
