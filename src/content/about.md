In recent decades, the humanities have theorised what images are and what they do, from Mitchell’s pictorial turn and Boehm’s iconic turn to Bredekamp’s Bildakt, Belting’s anthropology and Elkins’s critical scepticism. Yet little attention has been paid to what researchers do with images when they think. When a scholar looks at a colonial retablo or a video game screenshot, we rarely track when the image enters the reasoning process: whether it triggers it, intervenes midway, or is simply added as illustration. This zone of tacit competence hides valuable knowledge about what we learn about, through and with images.

If vision-language models are to become partners in humanistic inquiry rather than mere classifiers, we first need an account of humanistic visual reasoning in vivo. Rather than building a taxonomy of methods, which would treat thinking with images as applied theory, In the project *Visual Open Research Data for the Humanities (VORD 2)* we have been running “Show and Tell” workshops alongside the development of an AI tool for reading images. Each session works as epistemological fieldwork, a thick description of one researcher’s visual reasoning performed live: one researcher, one to three images, twenty to thirty minutes of unscripted reflection. The aim is not a definitive interpretation but to make explicit the choreography between the researcher’s gaze, their conceptual vocabulary and the image’s affordances. What does this process teach us, and how does it challenge the ways the algorithmic gaze has shaped our approach to digital images and archives?
